The random walk

Tomorrow's price is today's price times a random shock. We estimate how big those shocks are (volatility), draw thousands of paths, and keep the whole distribution of where the stock lands. Four engines disagree on purpose — the gap between them is the model risk.

Direction probability

A random walk says P(up) is near 50% — and on a high-vol name it is reliably a little under, because volatility drags the median below the mean. That is arithmetic, not a forecast. Anyone selling you a directional edge from a random walk is selling noise.

Where the edge actually is

Volatility is forecastable — the backtest at the bottom of this page measures how well. When the market's implied vol sits above the model's forecast you are paid to sell the range; when it sits below, you buy it. That is the trade this page is built to find.

Read the calibration section before sizing anything. Every probability here is only as good as the volatility forecast behind it, and the walk-forward test reports that error in vol points. Each structure is re-scored at that error in both directions — a trade that only works if the forecast is right is not an edge, it is leverage on an opinion.

Does it work? walk-forward

Every estimate below was made using only data available at the time. Three questions: can it call direction, can it forecast volatility, and is the whole distribution the right shape? The third is the one that decides whether the strike probabilities above can be taken at face value.

Direction — Brier score

Lower is better. A coin flip scores 0.250; beating it is the only evidence that direction is forecastable at all.

Volatility — forecast error

Mincer-Zarnowitz: a perfect forecast has slope 1 and intercept 0. Slope well below 1 means the forecast over-extrapolates spikes.

Distribution — interval coverage

Empirical below nominal means the wings are too thin, so tail strikes get reached more often than the model says.

Method & limits

What is estimated

Volatility from six realized estimators plus a GARCH(1,1) fitted by Gaussian quasi-MLE with variance targeting; tail thickness as a Student-t df on the standardized residuals. The GARCH gives the term structure — today's vol decaying toward its long-run level at the fitted half-life — which is the vol each expiry is actually priced off.

How paths are drawn

Daily steps in log space, with an exact Brownian-bridge correction so the running high and low reflect intraday extremes rather than just the closes. Simulation horizon and pricing year fraction both use trading days over 252, so simulated and Black-Scholes variance span exactly the same interval.

What the chain contributes

Implied vols are re-solved from the current mid quote rather than taken from the feed, which computes them off the last trade. Out-of-the-money quotes only build the smile. Every row carries its quote quality, and structures whose legs have no bid or a crossed market are never ranked.

What this cannot do

It cannot predict direction — the backtest is there to prove it rather than assert it. It does not model earnings jumps as scheduled events, so a simulation spanning an earnings date understates the gap. It has no order book, so fills, assignment and early exercise on American options are outside it. Positive EV means the model disagrees with the market; the market is not automatically the one that is wrong.

Research output, not investment advice. Options carry the risk of total loss of premium, and the short structures shown here can lose more than the capital committed.
